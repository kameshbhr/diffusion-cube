# **Diffusion Cube 2.0: Concept Note**

## Context

Cube is part of [100 Pathways](https://100pathways.com/), a global collective working to build 100 AI diffusion pathways by 2030\. It is one of several enablers in the initiative's portfolio, working alongside the others rather than as a complete solution on its own.

Cube 1.0 focused on collecting existing know-how as pathways and helping people explore them. Since then, our thinking has evolved, shaped by its early use and by further reflection: know-how must be further unbundled around the jobs of adoption to make it transferable, the ecosystem needs it as much as new adopters do (see Appendix). 

This note sets out Cube 2.0, built on this. It positions Cube as a companion that helps adopters get the jobs in their AI adoption journey done. As adopters use Cube, pathways take shape and grow, and Cube itself evolves based on how it is used.

## Objective

Cube exists to help organisations using AI for impact succeed faster, at lower cost and with less risk, by learning from and getting help from others who have done it before. It also aims to make adopters and the wider ecosystem collaborators in diffusion, sharing what they learn as they go so that each adoption makes the next one easier.

In doing so, Cube contributes to four objectives of 100 Pathways:

* growing the collective, so more organisations use AI for impact;  
* activating demand for impact use cases;  
* supporting adoption;  
* providing evidence of the collective's progress.

## The problem

AI models are no longer the main barrier to using AI for social impact. Know-how is.

**Adopters** struggle to know what to do, when and how to do it, and whether it is working. Often they don't know what to ask. Others have faced the same challenges, but that experience sits in people's heads, scattered documents and polished case studies that leave out what failed, and it is hard to tell whether it applies in a different setting. Adopters relearn lessons and repeat avoidable mistakes, and smaller organisations are hit hardest.

**Organisations supporting adopters** build know-how across many adoptions, but it reaches only the adopters they work with directly. They want their resources and funding to have impact beyond their own team and focus area, and to do more, more effectively, by learning from each other.

**Those who have done the work** want to tell others what they did, but have few ways to do so beyond polished case studies. Lived experience goes untold, and the next adopter starts from scratch.

## What Cube is

Cube is an AI adoption companion. It transforms individual lived experience into collective know-how that can transfer across use cases and contexts. It compounds the value of each experience by combining it with others to meet an adopter's needs in their own context, creating value greater than the sum of its parts.

Through ongoing dialogue, Cube helps adopters reflect on their experience, revisit decisions, recognise gaps and learn from setbacks. It builds an understanding of their goals, context, decisions, progress and challenges, and brings relevant experiences from others into the conversation. It connects adopters with people who have faced similar challenges, so they can learn directly from each other, and lets them share their own learnings as they go.

Cube is a companion, not an expert. It helps adopters define and navigate their own pathway, not copy the pathways they learn from. It does not hand down answers or judge an adoption's success.

For organisations supporting adopters, such as funders and development organisations, Cube makes their know-how travel further than the adopters they work with directly, connects them to the wider ecosystem, and lets them share what they did, compounding their impact.

### What it enables

For adopters, Cube helps them:

* **explore** what others have done, what worked, what didn't, and why;  
* **run a health check** on where their adoption stands, what needs attention, and what they might be overlooking;  
* **get proactive suggestions**, if required, when a stage changes, a job seems stalled or missed, a relevant new experience appears;  
* **reach people** who have faced similar challenges and learn directly from them.

For the 100 Pathways collective, Cube:

* **surfaces needs** from adopters' questions and from convenings, showing where demand outpaces available know-how or partners;  
* **surfaces evidence** of progress, with success metrics and stories that build confidence in diffusion;  
* **supports convenings and workshops** by pulling in relevant know-how, adopter needs and stories.

### The collective brain

Cube acts as the **collective brain** of 100 Pathways. It brings together know-how from many adoption journeys, and combines and synthesises it into **collective know-how** that can be applied in new contexts. Cube works as an extension of each organisation in the collective, adopters and those supporting them alike, not a replacement for their own know-how. Because it includes people, it is useful from day one, even while captured know-how is still thin.

### How it grows: an exchange, not a repository

Cube grows through exchange, not just collection. Know-how and people help adopters on their journeys. Adopters' questions and experiences, along with conversations at convenings and in implementations, produce new know-how, which in turn draws more adopters in.

Three things keep this cycle turning:

* **Using Cube is sharing.** Adopters tell Cube what is happening because it helps them. Their experiences become part of their organisation's memory without extra reporting, and with consent, part of the collective memory.  
* **Every question counts.** What adopters ask, even when Cube has no answer, shows where they are stuck and what know-how is missing. These signals are shared with the collective in aggregate.  
* **Conversations feed back.** Much of what adopters learn comes from conversations outside Cube: with each other, and at convenings and workshops. Bringing that know-how back into Cube, so it is not lost, closes the loop.

## Who it is for

* **Adopters:** governments, NGOs, social enterprises and other organisations working towards an impact purpose and goals.  
* **Organisations supporting adopters:** solution providers, funders, development organisations and ecosystem partners. They take part in adopters' journeys, often carry out some of the work, and hold know-how from many adoptions that Cube helps diffuse.

## What success looks like

Success is measured the way 100 Pathways measures diffusion: whether adoption gets faster, more affordable and more trusted for the next adopter.

Compared with similar adoptions that don't use Cube, an adopter using it should see:

1. Fewer hours spent researching what others did.  
2. Fewer weeks to get up to speed.  
3. Fewer months from the decision to start to the first real users.  
4. Less money wasted on avoidable mistakes and prolonged pilots.  
5. More adoptions achieving sustained impact beyond the pilot.

For the collective, Cube should show:

1. Connections between adopters that lead to useful conversations.  
2. Know-how from collective members reaching new adopters.  
3. Needs and gaps surfaced by Cube that shape new pathways, partnerships and convening agendas.

Targets will be set once Cube has early users to measure against.

## Design approach

Cube's design has two parts: the **jobs framework**, a common structure that defines the jobs of an adoption, and the **Pathway Model**, which defines how know-how is captured and organised. Together they make lived experience transferable: the jobs show that two experiences concern the same work, and the context in a pathway shows whether one applies to the other.

### Jobs framework

Cube is organised around jobs. A job is a committed set of tasks aimed at an outcome, such as a contracted vendor delivering the solution. Each job has an intended result, so Cube looks at whether outcomes are achieved, not only whether activities are done.

Each job is placed on three dimensions:

| Dimension | Question answered | Categories |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Stage | When is the job typically relevant? | Explore, Define, Pilot, Scale |
| Work Area | What aspect of adoption does it concern? | People and Problem, Solution, Institution, Ecosystem |
| Job Type | What kind of job is being done? | Discover, Decide, Deliver, Operate |

The framework lets Cube show adopters the jobs likely ahead, their intended results and the choices within them, even when they don't know what to ask. 

Stages are not a rigid sequence, and jobs may recur. The job list starts from collective experience and is refined as journeys are contributed. Details are in the Framework tab.

### Pathway Model: how know-how is captured

A **pathway** is a structured account of experiences and insights from an adoption journey that others can use.

Each pathway captures the **context** of an adoption (such as sector, people served, geography, capacity, infrastructure and regulation), its **outcomes**, the **jobs done** (what, why and how each job was done, decisions and reasons, setbacks, lessons and open questions), and **insights** that may cut across jobs.

Cube **synthesises learnings across pathways**: patterns seen in several adoptions, recording the journeys they draw on and how varied their contexts were. From these, it can help create **playbooks** for each use case. 

## Principles

1. **Say where it comes from.** Cube makes clear whether something comes from another adopter, from learnings across journeys, from the framework's guidance, or from its own reading of an adoption, and shares only what people agreed to share.  
2. **Don't overstate.** One adoption is one adoption. Cube says how proven know-how is, flags when it comes from a very different context, and says so when there is little or no evidence. Success stories meet the same standard, including what didn't work.  
3. **Honest, not flattering.** The health check says what it sees, especially when it's unwelcome.  
4. **A gap is a question.** Something not done may not have applied. Cube asks before drawing conclusions.  
5. **Every question counts.** A question Cube can't answer is still valuable: it shows what know-how the collective needs.  
6. **Easy to keep up to date.** Cube asks short questions, one at a time, and works with whatever an adopter can tell it, even if it's incomplete. Keeping Cube informed should feel like getting help, not submitting a report.

## Appendix: Hypotheses behind Cube 2.0

Cube 2.0 rests on the following hypotheses. Each will be tested as Cube 2.0 is used. Cube’s solution architecture and framework evolves based on how it is used.

1. **Know-how needs unbundling to be transferable.** Experience shared as whole stories is hard to reuse. Cube 1.0 used a basic framework to unbundle know-how into pathways; Cube 2.0 develops this into the jobs framework and the Pathway Model.  
2. **Adoption is a set of jobs.** AI adoption work is a set of broken-down jobs, done to make progress towards a purpose and goals through a set of use cases. Cube 2.0 is organised around these jobs.  
3. **The ecosystem needs know-how too.** Learnings are needed not only by new adopters but by the organisations helping them, and for diffusion as a whole. Cube 2.0 serves both.  
4. **Cube must help with specific jobs.** A bot that responds to queries is not enough. Cube needs to help adopters and the ecosystem with the specific jobs they have to do, which is why Cube 2.0 is a companion built around those jobs.
